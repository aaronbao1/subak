# 08 — Pooling, forecasting granularity, and changing populations

Research date: 2026-10-24. This is a new evidence review, not an implementation or an executed forecast experiment. Scope is company/team FP&A forecasts for human teams and human-initiated agents. Individual modeling is a candidate internal representation, not a commitment to individual budgets or UI.

## Decision and requirement trace

**Proposed design:** preserve five genuinely competing designs through evaluation: direct aggregate forecasting, independent individual forecasts summed, pooled individual forecasts summed, aggregate forecasting with individual-derived features, and reconciled forecasts from several levels. Start the comparison with simple regularized numerical models; complexity and granularity must earn their operational cost. Do not assume that accurate person forecasts are necessary for accurate team forecasts, or that team aggregation erases all useful person information.

## Established evidence and its limits

**Local versus global.** Montero-Manso and Hyndman prove that unrestricted global and local algorithms can reproduce the same forecasts on a finite collection when the forecasting input includes the whole observed series and the local learning rule is the same. This is an existence result, not a performance guarantee. Their section 2.2 explicitly removes the equivalence guarantee when both use restricted recent history: different full histories can have identical recent lags. Their generalization comparison uses bounded loss, an effective sample size for dependent observations, and independent series for the panel-average result; tighter bounds do not guarantee lower realized error. Therefore, correlated coworkers and finite-window learners require empirical testing rather than a claim that global models dominate. [Primary paper, v3, 26 March 2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2008.00444v3).

**Partial pooling.** A hierarchical regression lies between one shared coefficient vector and separately estimated group coefficients. Group coefficients share a population distribution; estimated variation and priors determine shrinkage. Varying slopes can accompany varying intercepts. This supplies an estimable way to retain heterogeneity, but the amount of pooling depends on likelihood and prior choices. [Stan User’s Guide 2.40, hierarchical regression](https://mc-stan.org/docs/stan-users-guide/regression.html#hierarchical-regression).

**Heterogeneity can hurt sharing.** Wu, Zhang and Ré analyze shared representations with task-specific outputs and show that task-data misalignment can cause negative transfer. Their theory is for specified linear/ReLU models; experiments are language/image tasks, not enterprise spending. More directly relevant, Godahewa et al. compare clustered and specialist ensembles of global forecasters on eight forecasting datasets and find gains over baseline global and univariate methods. These findings justify testing localized sharing; neither establishes that job titles identify the right clusters. [Multi-task study, ICLR 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00944); [localized forecasting, v2, 21 September 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15059v2).

**Shared parameters are different from shared shocks.** DeepAR learns a probabilistic autoregressive network across many related series. Deep Factors combines global latent structure with local probabilistic components and motivates its decomposition through exchangeable-series assumptions. They are concrete global/hybrid candidates, not proof that either is suitable for sparse enterprise histories. In particular, sharing fitted parameters alone must not be treated as a specification of the joint predictive dependence among coworkers. [DeepAR, v3, 22 February 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110v3); [Deep Factors, ICML 2019](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/wang19k.html).

**Crossed effects and reconciliation are separate tools.** The lme4 authors support nested and crossed random effects in linear/generalized mixed models, demonstrating that a single organization tree is not a required statistical structure. MinT reconciliation adjusts base forecasts to satisfy aggregation constraints; its optimality concerns the stated linear reconciliation setting, forecast-error covariance and unbiasedness conditions, not universal business accuracy. [lme4 author documentation, displayed version 1.1-37.9000](https://lme4.github.io/lme4/); [Wickramasuriya, Athanasopoulos and Hyndman, 2019](https://robjhyndman.com/publications/mint/).

## Representation is not estimation

**Mathematical distinction.** Let the additive target be team variable charge `Y = Σ_i Y_i`, on one currency, reporting basis and horizon. Let `F` be all admissible information known at forecast issue time. Then `E[Y | F] = Σ_i E[Y_i | F]`. A direct team model and a bottom-up model can therefore target the same conditional mean. This identity does not say that separately fitted models using only each person's own information equal a model using all information. For aggregate variance, covariance terms remain: `Var(Σ_i Y_i | F) = Σ_i Var(Y_i | F) + 2Σ_{i<j} Cov(Y_i,Y_j | F)`. Quantiles generally do not sum.

**Representational superiority** means a specified input/function class can express a useful relationship that another cannot. With squared loss and finite second moments, the ideal conditional expectation using a richer information set has no larger Bayes risk; strict improvement requires additional predictive information. That says nothing about finite-data fitting. A person roster can distinguish two teams with identical past totals when one loses its dominant user next week. Conversely, an aggregate model supplied with the same roster and rich permutation-invariant person features may represent that relationship too. Comparing a rich bottom-up model against an aggregate model deliberately denied roster information confounds granularity with information access.

**Estimation superiority** means an actual fitted procedure has lower out-of-sample decision loss for the target population and horizon at an acceptable operational cost. Extra person/project/model effects may have useful representation but be weakly identified, unstable, or expensive. Summing many noisy local fits can beat or lose to fitting a smoother aggregate. There is no general ordering without assumptions about signal, noise, dependencies, loss and sample size.

## Five competing designs

The comparisons below are **inferences and proposed designs**, not established enterprise results.

| Design | Inputs and learning unit | Plausible advantage | Principal failure and operational burden | Evidence that would overturn its case |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Direct aggregate | Team/company daily target, calendar, aggregate known plans; local per team or pooled across teams | Directly optimizes finance target; tolerates absent person attribution; few predictions to maintain | Few independent series; composition shifts hidden; pooled team model can still transfer badly | B/C/D deliver material gains on unseen roster changes at matching data coverage |
| B. Local individual then sum | Separately fitted user histories, individual calendar/context where available; explicit unassigned component | Distinct stable behavior; no forced parameter sharing; easy replacement of one user's forecast | Many short/intermittent histories, new-user failures, fitting/monitoring overhead, correlated misses | Aggregate-target loss or coverage consistently worsens versus A/D, especially for cold starts |
| C. Pooled individual then sum | Shared model with role/project/model features; partial pooling or local adaptations; common-shock mechanism for draws | Borrow strength across short histories while retaining heterogeneity; composition is explicit | Negative transfer, high-volume dominance, identity memorization, incorrect independent sampling | Heldout-user/team performance fails to beat A/D or subgroup errors rise despite average gains |
| D. Aggregate with individual features | Team target plus roster counts, usage concentration, tenure distribution, recent-user activity and model-mix summaries | Uses composition without requiring every individual forecast to be right; direct objective | Feature summaries can erase decisive interactions; leakage from future roster/activity; still requires person coverage | C improves materially with the same raw information and tuning budget; gain survives summary enrichment |
| E. Reconciled multilevel | Base forecasts at company/team and selected lower levels, versioned aggregation operator, residual covariance estimates | Combines signals at several resolutions and produces coherent outputs | Covariance estimation weak with few origins; changing memberships; negative values or excessive corrections | A/C coherent baselines match accuracy more cheaply; reconciliation amplifies bias or worsens intervals |

Do not equate A with local forecasting or C with complete pooling. A can pool teams; C can use hierarchical Bayes, shared trunks with local heads, clustered specialists, or shared regression plus local residuals. Reconciliation enforces arithmetic, whereas pooling shares information during estimation. They can coexist but neither implies the other.

## Choosing the learning unit and sharing structure

**Proposed model family:** begin with regularized additive/crossed effects rather than immediately building a user×project×task×model Cartesian panel. For an observed unit of usage, a candidate predictor is `g(E[U]) = calendar + company + team + person + project + model + selected interactions`, with an exposure term only when its measurement supports the assumed rate relationship. A hurdle/two-part outcome can separate probability of any activity from positive usage magnitude. Distribution and zero handling belong to the target's measurement process; missing coverage is not a zero.

A concrete hierarchical Bayes candidate gives each person's coefficient vector a population distribution, for example `theta_i ~ Normal(B z_i, Sigma_person)`, with separate centered team/project effects and regularizing priors on variation. Richer data can support varying calendar or adoption slopes; a random intercept alone cannot express those differences. Posterior draws should include uncertainty in the population parameters, especially with few teams. Check identifiability, posterior predictive behavior and sensitivity to shrinkage priors before interpreting effects. Penalized mixed models offer a cheaper comparator; neural multitask models offer a shared representation with specialized outputs, but neither earns default status by name.

Person, team, project, task and model answer different questions. Person effects may capture persistent tool use; team effects shared calendar and management; project effects launches and bursts; task classes workflow intensity; model mix token/price composition. A person can work on several projects and models, and a project can cross teams. Consequently these are often crossed effects, not five nested levels. Task classes are normally reusable categories, while ephemeral task IDs may have virtually no repeated history. Adding an unidentifiable person×project interaction cannot reveal which one caused the change when assignments never vary.

Keep observation attribution separate from statistical effects. Represent spend at a mutually exclusive ledger grain and use documented allocation weights for split ownership. Provider projects or shared keys are not people. Preserve an unassigned/shared-service component; forcing every charge through a roster model would systematically omit production or centrally operated activity. Predict variable usage separately from known fees and explicit pricing assumptions. Correlated model substitution and usage intensity may require joint scenarios; multiplying independently predicted usage and price/mix can be biased.

For **few long series**, regularized team models and local models are serious contenders; a large global network has little independent panel information. For **many short series**, shared coefficients and hierarchical population distributions are plausible starting points, but total row count exaggerates information when employees share launches, outages, or repeated cumulative telemetry. For **few short series**, all designs are prior-sensitive: simple baselines, broad uncertainty, and conditional scenarios are more honest than elaborate hierarchy. These are hypotheses about suitability, not minimum-sample thresholds.

For **cold starts**, separate (1) a new user in a known role/team, (2) a known user moving teams/projects, (3) a new team of known users, and (4) an unseen company. A new individual receives a predictive population distribution conditional on available attributes, not a fitted effect equal to zero with certainty. Small local histories gradually update that distribution. Predicting a new company from other companies requires an explicitly permitted and representative training population; it cannot be inferred from one-company validation. If cross-company learning is unavailable, evaluate cold-start priors as unvalidated scenarios.

## Roster changes and forecast accounting

**Proposed contract:** freeze the as-of roster, identity mapping, cost basis and source coverage with each forecast. Store effective intervals and when each change became known. Historical as-incurred team totals and a restatement under the current organization are different targets. Never silently rewrite one into the other. Future planned allocations define a horizon-specific aggregation operator; a fixed operator is insufficient for a user moving teams during the horizon.

Maintain entrant, continuing, departing, transferred and unresolved populations. Dates of hires/leaves are known inputs only if known at issue time; uncertain hires need scenario distributions or explicit scenario branches. A departure does not necessarily remove the workload: successors, shared agents or project reassignment may preserve team spending. Carrying personal spending into a new team without considering project continuity is equally unsafe. Unknown entrants require a residual population component; forecasts that sum only today's named people assume a closed roster.

For probabilistic bottom-up or reconciled outputs, aggregate joint draws across people, days and relevant model mix before calculating cumulative 7/14/30-day and calendar-remainder quantiles. Include uncertainty in local effects, shared shocks and uncertain roster scenarios. Independently drawing each individual can misstate team tail risk even when individual marginal intervals look calibrated.

## Proposed falsification and evaluation protocol

No experiments have been executed. Freeze target definition, forecast origin, coverage rules and source availability before running the following:

1. **Five-arm rolling-origin comparison.** Evaluate all five designs on team/company cumulative variable-charge targets, with known fees added through the same deterministic component. Use matching observed records, horizons, retraining cadence and tuning budgets. First compare with each design's natural information; then run matched-information ablations that give A/D roster/context access, exposing information gains separately from estimation structure.
2. **Population splits.** Evaluate known-user future periods; completely heldout users with 0/3/7/14 days of permitted adaptation history; heldout teams; and heldout companies. Team holdouts must account for people who transfer across teams. Report both strict new-entity tests and transfer-of-known-person tests. Keep all outcomes beyond each issue time unavailable, including documents and roster revisions. Fit normalizers, clusters, priors, selection rules and reconciliation covariance only within the training fold.
3. **Learning curves and heterogeneity.** Vary number of people, independent teams and history length separately. Report role/project/provider/model, tenure, region/calendar, activity sparsity and spending-concentration slices, plus negative-transfer rate: the fraction of targets whose loss increases when another population is added. Compare complete pooling, hierarchical effects, identity-free features, clusters and local corrections. A mean gain does not excuse a reliably harmed important team.
4. **Identity perturbations.** A consistent bijective renaming of opaque IDs, with the mapping and learned state permuted together, must preserve predictions within numerical tolerance. Mask IDs to test deployability for unseen people. Shuffle histories among identities within controlled cohorts as a diagnostic ablation, not an invariance requirement. Split/merge duplicate identities while preserving ledger totals to measure modeling sensitivity and expose counting bugs; target accounting must remain invariant although fitted forecasts may change. Test recycled emails as new identities.
5. **Calendar and roster interventions.** Hold total headcount fixed while changing composition; move the largest user; replace a departing user on the same project; create a new team from known users; mix a hiring burst with a new-model adoption. Compare frozen-roster continuation against known-roster-change scenarios. Such synthetic perturbations test mechanisms, not real-world forecast accuracy.
6. **Score the actual decision.** Use team/company bias and dollar MAE or another agreed point loss; assess cumulative quantile loss, interval coverage/sharpness and budget-exceedance calibration where probabilities are supported. Report equal-team and spend-weighted results alongside critical-team slices. Use dependence-aware uncertainty on performance differences rather than treating person-days as independent. Include compute, attribution completeness, maintenance burden and unavailable-forecast frequency.

**Unvalidated hypotheses:** partial pooling will improve new-user performance; D will capture much of the benefit of C at lower operating cost; project and model effects will outperform persistent personal identity during reorganizations; localized sharing will protect unusual research teams; joint common-shock draws will improve aggregate interval calibration. Any hypothesis can be rejected by the protocol. Until representative labeled enterprise data exists, retain these as candidates and expose the chosen method and coverage limits in forecast metadata.
